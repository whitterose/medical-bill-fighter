1. Get your action plan

Answer what you can, then tap the button. You can skip any question you're not sure about.

Who sent the bill?
Did you use health insurance for this care?
Was this an emergency room visit, or an ambulance ride by air?
Did a doctor you didn't choose bill you separately as "out-of-network"? (This is common with anesthesiologists, radiologists, pathologists, and ER doctors.)
Did your insurer deny the claim, or say a service wasn't covered?
If you were uninsured, did you get a "Good Faith Estimate" before scheduled care, and is the bill much higher?
Does your household income make this bill hard or impossible to pay?
Does the bill show each charge separately, with codes?

2. Check your bill for common errors

Type in or paste the lines from your itemized bill. This only flags things worth asking about. It can't tell you for sure that a charge is wrong.

You can separate columns with commas, tabs, or |. Column order: date, code, description, quantity, amount. Columns you don't have can be left blank, e.g. , , Lab work, 1, 85.

4. What to say on the phone

Write down the date, time, and name of everyone you talk to. Ask them to note on your account that you're reviewing the bill.

Ask for the itemized bill
"Hi, I'm calling about account number ___. Before I pay, I need a fully itemized bill showing every charge, with the billing codes, dates, and quantities. Can you mail or email that to me? And can you please put a hold on the account while I review it?"
Ask about financial assistance (charity care)
"Does this hospital have a financial assistance policy? I'd like an application, the income limits, and a plain-language summary. I'd like to apply for this bill and any others from this hospital. Please don't send the account to collections while my application is being reviewed."

Nonprofit hospitals must have a written financial assistance policy, and many for-profit hospitals have one too. You don't have to be "poor" to qualify. Some policies cover middle-income families.

Ask for a self-pay discount
"I'm paying for this myself. What's your self-pay or prompt-pay discount on this balance? If I can't pay it all at once, what interest-free payment plan can you offer?"
When a charge looks wrong
"I'm looking at line ___, code ___, on ___. I don't believe I received this service (or it's listed twice). Please review it and send me a corrected bill. Will you hold the account while that's reviewed?"
When a collector calls
"I don't recognize this debt as valid yet. Please send me the written validation notice. I'll respond in writing."
(Don't confirm bank or card details, and don't agree to pay on the call.)

If a caller pushes you to pay right away by gift card, wire, or crypto, it's almost certainly a scam. Hang up and call the hospital using the number on its official website.

5. Know your rights (US)

This is a short summary with links to official sources. Rules change, and states often add more protections.

Surprise bills (No Surprises Act)

Since January 1, 2022, most insured people are protected from surprise out-of-network bills for emergency care, for some non-emergency care at in-network hospitals and surgery centers, and for air ambulances. If you're uninsured or paying yourself, you can usually get a Good Faith Estimate, and you may be able to dispute a bill that's at least $400 more than the estimate.

Source: CMS: Medical bill rights. Help line: 1-800-985-3059.

Nonprofit hospitals: financial assistance

Nonprofit hospitals must have a written financial assistance policy (FAP). Before taking "extraordinary collection actions" (like credit reporting or lawsuits), they have to make reasonable efforts to see whether you qualify. They generally must accept your application for at least 240 days after the first bill after you leave the hospital. If you're approved, they have to try to reverse any collection actions and refund what you overpaid.

Source: IRS: Section 501(r)(6).

Debt collectors must prove it

If you dispute a debt in writing within 30 days of getting the collector's validation notice, the collector has to stop collecting until it sends you verification.

Source: CFPB; 15 U.S.C. § 1692g.

Insurance denials can be appealed

You generally have 180 days from the denial notice to file an internal appeal. For care you've already received, the insurer usually has to decide within 60 days. If it still says no, you can ask for an external review.

Source: HealthCare.gov: Internal appeals.

Credit reports

Under their own voluntary policies, the three big credit bureaus leave off paid medical collections, medical collections under $500, and unpaid medical collections that are less than a year old. A broader federal rule was struck down in July 2025, so these are company policies, not federal law, and they can change. Some states have stronger laws.

Sources: CFPB notice on the vacated rule; CFPB blog.

Why it's worth checking

In a KFF survey, 43% of adults said they had gotten a medical or dental bill they believed had an error. In a 2024 study, 74% of people who contacted the billing office about a concern got the mistake fixed.

Sources: PIRG, citing KFF; AARP, citing JAMA Health Forum (2024).

6. Free help from real people

  • Dollar For is a nonprofit that helps patients apply for hospital charity care for free: dollarfor.org
  • No Surprises help desk (CMS): 1-800-985-3059. You can also file a complaint at cms.gov/medical-bill-rights
  • Your state's Consumer Assistance Program can help with insurance appeals, and can even file one for you: see HealthCare.gov
  • Problems with a debt collector? File a complaint with the CFPB: consumerfinance.gov/complaint
  • Free legal aid if you're being sued: LawHelp.org. Never ignore court papers. Missing a deadline can lead to an automatic judgment against you.
